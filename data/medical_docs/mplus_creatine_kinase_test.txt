What is a creatine kinase (CK) test?

A creatine kinase test measures the amount of creatine kinase (CK) in a sample of your blood. CK is also called creatine phosphokinase (CPK).

CK is an enzyme that helps your muscles restore their energy supply. An enzyme is a protein that speeds up certain chemical reactions in your body. There are three types of this enzyme:

- CK-MM, which is stored mostly in your skeletal muscles (the muscles you need to move)

- CK-MB, which is stored mostly in your heart muscle

- CK-BB, which is stored mostly in your brain tissue

Most of the CK in your body is CK-MM.

Normally, a small amount of CK gets into your blood from the usual wear and tear on your muscles. But if your muscles, heart, or brain tissues are damaged, larger amounts of CK leak out of your cells into your bloodstream.

Other names: CK, total CK, creatine phosphokinase, CPK

What is it used for?

A CK test is mainly used to help diagnose and monitor injuries and diseases that damage skeletal muscles and cause high levels of CK in your blood.

If you've had an injury to your muscles, your health care provider may check your CK levels more than once over the course of a few days. That's because CK levels may not reach their highest for up to two days after certain injuries. By testing more than once, your provider can see if your levels change.

Injuries can include those from:

- Accidents, such as car crashes, that crush, compress, tear, or strain your muscles

- Serious burns

- Extreme exercise

Muscle diseases that can damage your skeletal muscles include:

- Muscular dystrophy, a group of inherited (passed down through families) muscle diseases that weaken muscles over time.

- Rhabdomyolysis, or “rhabdo,” a rapid breakdown of muscle tissue which releases proteins and electrolytes into the blood. This uncommon condition can damage the heart and lead to sudden kidney failure. Rhabdo has many causes, including serious injuries, working in very hot places, overusing muscles, and certain medicines and health conditions that affect the muscles.

- Myositis, a group of rare diseases that involve long-term muscle inflammation (swelling), weakness, and sometimes pain.

Why do I need a CK test?

You may need a CK test if you have symptoms of a muscular disorder. These include:

- Muscle pain or cramps

- Muscle weakness

- Balance problems or frequent falls

- Numbness or tingling

- Dark colored urine (pee)

- Swollen legs or feet

You may also need this test if you:

- Have had an injury that crushed or tore your muscles

- Have had serious burns

- Take certain medicines that may cause muscle damage, such as statins to lower blood cholesterol

- Have had a stroke or traumatic brain injury (TBI)

What happens during a CK test?

A health care professional will take a blood sample from a vein in your arm, using a small needle. After the needle is inserted, a small amount of blood will be collected into a test tube or vial. You may feel a little sting when the needle goes in or out. This usually takes less than five minutes.

Will I need to do anything to prepare for the test?

You don't need any special preparations for a CK test. To help make sure your results are accurate, your provider may ask you to avoid intense exercise and drinking alcohol for a few days before your test.

Are there any risks to the test?

There is very little risk to having a blood test. You may have slight pain or bruising at the spot where the needle was put in, but most symptoms go away quickly.

What do the results mean?

A healthy amount of CK varies depending on:

- Your age, sex, and race

- How much muscle you have

- How physically active you are

Because of this, your provider will explain whether your results are outside of or within a range that is healthy for you.

If your CK level is higher than is healthy for you, it usually means you have some type of muscle damage. Your provider may then order more specific types of CK tests to find out if the CK is coming from your muscles, heart, or brain. These more specific tests are called CK isoenzymes tests, and they can check for:

- High levels of CK-MM enzymes, which would most likely mean that you have some damage to your skeletal muscles.

- High levels of CK-MB enzymes, which would most likely mean that you have some damage to your heart muscle. The most common cause is a heart attack or inflammation in your heart muscle (myocarditis), but other conditions can also cause high levels of CK-MB. These include:

- A blood clot in the lung

- Heart surgery

- Congestive heart failure

- Hypothyroidism

- Kidney failure

- Higher than normal CK-BB enzymes, which may mean that you had a stroke or brain injury. However, it could also mean you have a condition that affects smooth muscles. Smooth muscles are muscles you can't control, such as those in your digestive system. If your provider suspects a stroke, they may use a CK-BB test to see how severe a stroke was and whether you'll have another one.

Though a CK-MB test can show if there is damage to your heart muscle, providers don't often use it to confirm or rule out a heart attack. That's because troponin tests are better at finding the damage a heart attack causes the heart muscle. Usually a CK-MB test is only used if a troponin test isn't available.

If you had multiple CK tests, and your levels were high for each one, it may mean that muscle damage is continuing to happen.

A CK test result on its own can't show where the damage is or what's causing it. To do this, your provider will consider your symptoms, medical history, and the results from other tests to understand what your test results mean.

If you have questions about any of your test results, talk with your provider.
